From pixels to policy — an operating system for neighborhood governance

Thailand's local governance still plans informal-settlement policy with data that is years or decades old. This proposal turns THEOS-2 satellite pixels and daily public signals into a live neighborhood score, and pairs every score profile with a ready-made policy kit drawn from proven upgrading programs worldwide.

1 · Main message

Cities change every day; the data that governs them does not. Bangkok's 626 official slum polygons, the NHA low-income community registry, and decadal census tables describe a city that in places is up to twenty years old. A fire last night, a new row of roofs this monsoon, a street market that moved — none of it reaches the desk that allocates upgrading budgets.

Today: policy on stale data

✔ One-off surveys, some from the 2000s

✔ Census every 10 years

✔ Complaints answered case-by-case

✘ No measure of change between surveys

✘ Budgets chase the loudest voice, not the worst conditions

→

Proposal: policy on a live score

✔ Daily signals: complaints, news, public social media (92-day rolling window)

✔ Statistical baselines: registries, census, plan zones, flood layers

✔ Per-neighborhood score, refreshed continuously

✔ Score profile maps to a costed policy kit with a named precedent

✔ Satellite segmentation audits the result from orbit

Why daily + statistical must be fused

Daily signals alone are biased toward loud, connected, Thai-literate neighborhoods; statistics alone are stale. The score uses statistics as the denominator and baseline, daily signals as the change detector, and never ranks communities on a single source. Communities with weak signal get an explicit data-blindness flag instead of a falsely clean score — an anti-stigmatization guardrail (see §6, Q3).

4 · Policy kits — assembled from proven programs

Each kit is a package a district office can actually launch: a named precedent, standard measures, a budget band, and the score signature that triggers it. Precedents come from real upgrading programs; scale figures are approximate, cited for orientation.

KIT 1 · Collective in-situ upgrading (บ้านมั่นคง 2.0)

Baan Mankong, CODI Thailand — from 2003; community-planned upgrading (on-site, re-blocking, reconstruction, land sharing) with infrastructure subsidy + collective loans to community organizations; reported 100,000+ households across phases.

Collective tenure negotiation (lease/CO-op) + infrastructure subsidy + housing credit, planned and managed by the community savings group. 9–24 months.

Trigger: stable settlement, high complaint backlog, willing CBO, landowner identifiable (registry + plan-zone layers confirm site is not in a sensitive zone §5).

KIT 2 · Minimal re-blocking + infrastructure

Kampung Improvement Program, Jakarta — from 1969, governor-led, later World Bank-supported; paved footpaths, drainage, water points, toilets, schools/health posts; reached millions of residents nationwide.

Fire lanes cut through dense fabric, drainage, water points, lighting, waste collection. Housing stays resident-built. Cheapest kit; visible in weeks.

Trigger: satellite roof density > threshold (§5) + fire/technical-disaster events + narrow-lane fabric on imagery.

KIT 3 · Land sharing / readjustment

Land-sharing instruments in Thai practice (part of the Baan Mankong menu) and Korean-style land readjustment; also the core logic of Mukuru SPA, Nairobi — 2017, ≈645 acres, ~100k households, county + Muungano Alliance consortium planning.

Deal with landowner: part of the plot commercialized, part secured for residents on-site. Requires cadastre check + value appraisal; longest kit (2–5 years).

Trigger: high land value (plan zone = commercial/mixed) + eviction pressure signal in news + viable density for partial relocation on same plot.

KIT 4 · Risk-first retrofit

Favela-Bairro, Rio de Janeiro — 1994–2000s, IDB-financed (≈US$180M first phase): infrastructure + public space to integrate favelas into the formal city; and Medellín PUI (2004–), cable transit + library-parks as anchor investments.

Flood-proofing, fire breaks, slope works, evacuation routes; anchored on the BMA risk registry + GISTDA repeated-flood layer, cross-checked against daily disaster events.

Trigger: natural-disaster sub-score high + community inside repeated-flood WMS zone or FY-2568 risk registry.

KIT 5 · Street-economy legitimization

Street-vendor licensing + popup-market programs in Bangkok and international variants (e.g., Medellín and Seoul night-market regularization).

Vending permits, market days, sanitation + waste service for popup markets, micro-credit. Turns the strongest daily signal (street retail events) into taxable, safer activity.

Trigger: street-retail/popup sub-score high AND vending-related complaints rising — vitality with friction, not repression.

KIT 6 · Relocation with common-use dividend

CODI relocations (part of Baan Mankong); contrast case: evictions without re-homing historically deepened poverty (documented for pre-Baan-Mankong Bangkok clearances).

Last resort for unsafe land. Package must include: near-site units, transport continuity, and a rebuilt common-use area on the vacated land (see §6 Q2). No bare relocation.

Trigger: settlement inside sensitive zone (§5) with no retrofit option + disaster exposure — never triggered by land value alone.

5 · Companion tool: segmentation of slums and sensitive areas

The second tool in the stack is a computer-vision layer that reads THEOS-2 imagery (0.5 m) directly, independent of any registry — so it also sees settlements the registries missed or dropped.

What exists today (working prototypes on this site)

  • Try 2 (SAM) — first segmentation attempt on THEOS-2 blocks (superseded by Gen 1).
  • Gen 1: SAM roofs — dense 48-point/side grid prompt sweep: 5,627 roof polygons, 5,620 after regularization (straight edges, median bbox-fill 1.00).
  • Gen 2: U-Net roofs — student U-Net trained on the Gen 1 teacher, IoU 0.542 vs teacher, runs without prompts at scale.
  • Slum map — 1,588 NHA communities × plan zones; the registry view the segmentation is benchmarked against.
  • Slum policies review — 16-country comparison of slum policies and outcomes.

6 · Open questions — we take a position, you decide

Q1 · Should the governor come to the slum before starting a policy?

For: Baan Mankong succeeded because planning started in community meetings, not offices — field presence built the trust that made collective loans bankable. A visit ground-truths a score that may rest on T3 (district-level) matches. Enumeration visits historically produce the data that no census collects.

Against: a single visit without protocol becomes a photo-op; it raises expectations, distorts the daily signal for weeks (event categories spike around VIP visits), and can intimidate exactly the households the policy is meant to reach.

Our position: yes — but as a structured enumeration visit, not a ceremony: community leaders + district officers + scorecard in hand, one walking audit, findings written back into the dashboard within 48 hours. The score triggers the visit; the visit re-scores the neighborhood.

Q2 · Should rebuilt areas be provided to slum dwellers for common use?

For: relocation and upgrading fail politically when residents see only losses. A common-use dividend — the vacated or redeveloped parcel returned as a market hall, playground, community center or productive land held by the community cooperative — converts policy from extraction into investment. Precedent logic: Medellín's library-parks and public-space anchors made upgrading legitimate; CODI's collective title includes common land by design.

Against: common space on valuable land is costly; maintenance responsibility is unclear without a cooperative structure; poorly used commons can decay into re-occupation.

Our position: yes, as a standing component of KIT 6 (and optional in KIT 1/3): every relocation/upgrading package reserves a share of the rebuilt area as community-held common use, with the cooperative as manager. The segmentation tool (§5) proposes candidate parcels — vacated footprints large enough to be useful.

Q3 · Can a live neighborhood score stigmatize the neighborhoods it scores?

A public "worst slums" ranking invites redlining by insurers, employers and landlords. The same daily-signal pipeline also touches PDPA questions (complaints are individual records; we strip PII at ingestion, but re-identification risk grows with resolution).

Our position: the score is an investment-priority instrument for the administration, not a public label. Published form: budget-allocation tiers and positive-action lists, never a ranked league table. Data-blindness flags are published alongside so quiet communities are not read as problem-free.
